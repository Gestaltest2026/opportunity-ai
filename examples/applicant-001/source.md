# Applicant 001 — Source Materials

## Source 1: Personal Statement

What first drew me to legal study was not the courtroom, but the quiet fact that other people's lives can be altered by the precision (or imprecision) of ordinary work. After graduating from high school in Tokyo in 1998 and completing junior college in 2000, I began my professional life in administrative roles in Japan. There I learned that institutions do not function through policy alone. They depend on accuracy, accountability, and the daily discipline of carrying responsibility for others. That early understanding still shapes how I approach law: not as an abstract set of rules, but as a structure that can either protect people or fail them depending on how carefully it is sustained.

I later encountered that same truth from another direction. In 2007, I founded my own Hawaiian hula school in Japan, which I continue to operate online. Teaching students whose ages and life circumstances differed widely showed me that continuity matters as much as instruction. What kept people returning was not only the technique of dance, but the stability of a shared practice—one with rhythm, expectations, and mutual commitment. I saw that participation over time could create belonging. My volunteer work with elderly residents, children, and individuals with disabilities deepened that understanding and led me to see support not simply as kindness, but as the creation of environments in which dignity, connection, and steady participation can take root.

Raising my child on my own sharpened this perspective further. Single parenthood taught me to distinguish between control and guidance. I learned that correction without consistency rarely changes behavior in lasting ways, and that growth cannot be forced simply by authority. It must be supported through example, restraint, and respect for individual development. That lesson now informs the way I understand human behavior: durable outcomes are shaped not only by rules and consequences, but by the quality of guidance and structure that precede them.

After moving to Florida about seven years ago, I studied English through an ESOL program and then joined a Florida law office as a Legal Assistant, later advancing to Paralegal and Office Manager. In legal practice, I began to see a pattern that clarified my goals. By the time formal legal intervention becomes necessary, communication has often broken down, obligations have been neglected, and trust has eroded. Legal action matters, but it often begins only after damage has become difficult to undo. That realization moved my interest beyond administration and into legal study itself. I want to understand not only how the law responds to conflict, but how institutions, education, and guidance can help prevent conflict from hardening into crisis.

At the same time, I remained committed to formal study. Through distance education at Nihon University, I studied English literature, which strengthened my ability to read closely, interpret language carefully, and think about the tension between individual lives and social judgment. I am now studying Legal Studies at Florida Gulf Coast University. My academic path has not been traditional, but it reflects persistence, discipline, and a deliberate effort to connect experience with intellectual growth.

This is why the University of Florida matters to me. I am seeking the University of Florida not merely because it is flexible, but because I need a rigorous academic environment in which my professional experience, community work, and long-term commitment to service can be brought into sharper analytical focus. I am particularly drawn to CJL 3038: Law and Society because it would allow me to study more rigorously the question that already shapes my work and my purpose: how institutions respond to human vulnerability, and how law can preserve dignity, responsibility, and possibility before harm becomes irreversible. UF Online would allow me to continue my legal work, family responsibilities, and community involvement while pursuing the disciplined study necessary for law school and for a career centered on prevention, advocacy, and public contribution.

What unites my experiences is one conviction: people are more likely to thrive when structure is paired with guidance, and when responsibility is supported by environments that preserve dignity. That is the question I want to keep pursuing, and it is the reason I am applying to the University of Florida.
